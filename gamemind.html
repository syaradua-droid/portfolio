GameMind: getting youth athletes the gear they need and a safe way to talk about pressure

Low-income youth athletes need equipment they can't always afford, and a way to talk about competitive stress that doesn't read as weakness. The hardest part wasn't the interface — it was making both feel safe to ask for.

Role
UX/UI Designer (Solo)
Platform
Web (Coordinator Portal) + Mobile (Athlete App)
Tools
Figma
Project Type
Self-Directed Concept

The problem, from two sides

GameMind is a self-directed concept — a problem I wanted to design for, not a client brief or a researched initiative. No interviews, no formal personas, no usability rounds behind it: just a system I wanted to think through end to end, imagined from two sides at once.

"Low-income youth athletes need equipment they can't always afford, and a way to talk about competitive stress that doesn't read as weakness."

Equipment gap

Gear requests, donor inventory, and fulfillment would otherwise happen informally — nothing tracking requests, matching them to donations, or showing an athlete where their gear actually is.

Stigma around asking

Requesting help — for equipment or for how competition pressure feels — carries a social cost for a teenager, especially in front of teammates and coaches.

No shared system

A coordinator and an athlete have no common tool: the coordinator working from memory and spreadsheets, the athlete with no visibility into a request once it leaves their hands.

Two roles, two very different jobs to be done

Same platform, opposite vantage points — a coordinator triaging requests at scale, and an athlete who just wants gear and a moment to reset before the pressure of the next practice. Coach Rivera and Jordan are illustrative, not research participants — a way to keep the design honest about who each screen was for.

ROLE A // PROGRAM COORDINATOR

Coach Rivera

Runs equipment logistics for the program on top of an already full coaching load. Needs to triage urgent requests, match them to donated inventory, and spot which athletes need a wellness check-in — fast, without digging.

ROLE B // YOUTH ATHLETE

Jordan

Wants gear without the awkwardness of asking a coach face-to-face, and somewhere to admit "I'm not feeling ready today" that doesn't get treated as an excuse.

From paper to product

Early sketching focused on the request flow first — the moment with the most social friction, since it's the one an athlete has to initiate themselves.

Early paper wireframe — request detail screen, before the anonymity toggle and confidential-hub framing were added

The coordinator portal

One dashboard for the side of GameMind Coach Rivera actually lives in — pending requests, available donated inventory, and which athletes have flagged high pre-game stress, all visible without opening a single spreadsheet.

Coordinator dashboard — urgency, status, and wellness alerts surfaced at a glance so nothing depends on memory

Three numbers that matter

Pending requests, available donated gear, and wellness check-in alerts sit at the top — the three things a coordinator needs before doing anything else.

Status, not spreadsheets

Every request carries urgency and status badges (Unmatched, Matched, Shipped, Ready for Pickup) so triage is a glance, not a lookup.

Donations stay visible

A donation drop-off log keeps community contributions traceable back to the requests they fulfill.

Design system

A dark, low-glare UI built for a coordinator scanning a dashboard between drills, and an athlete checking in from a phone at the gym. Status color does the heavy lifting so nobody has to read every label.

Primary Purple

Primary actions, active nav state, high-readiness fill

Amber

In-transit status, medium urgency, low-readiness prompt

Emerald

Matched / Ready for Pickup — the "it's handled" state

Urgent Red

High-urgency requests, low mental-readiness scores

Backgrounds, type scale, buttons, and form controls (dropdown, segmented category, size chips, privacy toggle) are documented as a shared sticker sheet in Figma, so the coordinator portal and athlete app read as one system despite serving two very different contexts of use.

The athlete app adapts to how you're actually feeling

A daily mental-readiness check-in drives what the home screen shows next — a low score surfaces a short reset before anything else competes for attention.

Low readiness — 3/10
Higher readiness — 7/10

Below a threshold, the reset card takes priority over everything else on the screen — Request Gear and Mindset Lab drop to secondary position. It's a small piece of logic, but it's the difference between an app that just logs a number and one that actually responds to it.

Mindset Lab

Short, specific mental-training exercises — filterable by what's actually happening (pre-game anxiety, handling pressure, focus) rather than one generic "wellness" bucket.

Exercises are named for the moment they're for — "Post-Error Recovery," "Championship Breathing" — not generic labels

Requesting gear, without the awkward conversation

A structured request replaces the DM-to-a-coach model — sport, category, and size as quick taps, an anonymity toggle so a name never has to reach a donor, and a status tracker so an athlete never has to ask "did that go through?"

Request form — anonymity is on by default, with a plain-language confidentiality note instead of legal boilerplate
Status tracker — a four-stage stepper and a QR pickup code close the loop without another conversation

Design decisions that came from the stigma problem

The interface work here was the easy part. The harder calls were about what the product should never require of someone.

What it had to solve forDesign response
Asking a coach in person for gear can feel like admitting you can't afford to play. Request Gear is a form, not a conversation — with an anonymity toggle on by default so a donor never sees a name.
A daily "how are you feeling" check-in only works if it changes something, not just logs a number. A low readiness score reorders the home screen itself — the reset becomes the first thing you see, not an optional add-on.
Coordinators can't act on wellness data they have to go looking for. Wellness Check-In Alerts sit on the coordinator dashboard next to equipment requests — same priority, same glance.

Takeaways & what's next

What I learned

Designing for two users on one platform means every screen has to earn its place twice — once for what a coordinator needs to act on, and once for what an athlete needs to feel safe enough to use at all.

Next steps

This is concept-stage: a design system and product flows built solo in Figma, with no client and no research behind it yet. Talking to actual coordinators and youth athletes is the next step before any of this could ship for real.
