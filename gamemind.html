GameMind

Getting low-income youth athletes the gear they need — and a safe way to talk about competitive pressure. The hardest part wasn't the interface; it was making both feel safe to ask for.

RoleUX/UI Designer (Solo)
PlatformWeb coordinator portal + mobile athlete app
ToolsFigma
TypeSelf-directed concept
The problem

Two sides, no shared system.

GameMind is a self-directed concept — a problem I wanted to design for, not a client brief. No interviews, personas or usability rounds behind it: a system I wanted to think through end to end, from two sides at once.

“Low-income youth athletes need equipment they can't always afford, and a way to talk about competitive stress that doesn't read as weakness.”

Equipment gap

Requests, donor inventory and fulfillment happen informally — nothing tracks a request, matches it to a donation, or shows an athlete where their gear is.

Stigma around asking

Requesting help — for equipment or for how pressure feels — carries a social cost for a teenager, especially in front of teammates and coaches.

No shared system

The coordinator works from memory and spreadsheets; the athlete has no visibility once a request leaves their hands.

Two roles

Same platform, opposite vantage points.

Coach Rivera and Jordan are illustrative, not research participants — a way to keep the design honest about who each screen was for.

Role A · Program coordinator

Coach Rivera

Runs equipment logistics on top of a full coaching load. Needs to triage urgent requests, match them to donated inventory, and spot which athletes need a wellness check-in — fast, without digging.

Role B · Youth athlete

Jordan

Wants gear without the awkwardness of asking a coach face-to-face, and somewhere to admit “I'm not feeling ready today” that doesn't get treated as an excuse.

Research & specification

Built for early intervention, not surveillance.

Four user stories anchored both sides of the platform — mapped here against the acceptance criteria that made each one buildable, then traced end-to-end through the athlete check-in & coordinator triage loop below.

User storyAcceptance criteria
Complete daily readiness check-in under 60 secondsHigh-contrast single-gesture sliders; sub-60s completion time; immediate post-check-in grounding exercise.
Trust data privacy and non-punitive intentPersistent privacy disclaimer detailing data governance: aggregated trends shared, personal logs private.
Spot acute roster stress spikes before burnout occursAutomated triage flags surface when 3-day rolling delta drops >25% below personal baseline.
Initiate proactive, confidential support intervention1-click confidential outreach scheduler to coordinate 1-on-1 support without public roster alerts.
From paper to product

Sketching the hardest moment first.

Why the request flow came first

It's the moment with the most social friction — the one an athlete has to initiate themselves. If asking for gear feels like admitting you can't afford to play, nothing else in the product matters. Every later screen was designed around making that first ask feel routine.

Coordinator portal

One dashboard, no spreadsheets.

Pending requests, donated inventory, and athletes who've flagged high pre-game stress — all visible without opening a single file.

Three numbers that matter

Pending requests, available donated gear, and wellness alerts sit at the top — what a coordinator needs before doing anything else.

Status, not spreadsheets

Every request carries urgency and status badges (Unmatched, Matched, Shipped, Ready for Pickup) so triage is a glance.

Donations stay visible

A drop-off log keeps community contributions traceable to the requests they fulfill.

Design system

Status color does the heavy lifting.

A dark, low-glare UI for a coordinator scanning between drills and an athlete checking in from the gym. Components live on one shared sticker sheet in Figma so both surfaces read as one system.

Primary Purple#7C3AEDPrimary actions, active nav, high-readiness fill
Amber#F59E0BIn-transit status, medium urgency, low-readiness prompt
Emerald#10B981Matched / Ready for Pickup — the “it's handled” state
Urgent Red#EF4444High-urgency requests, low mental-readiness scores
Athlete app

The home screen adapts to how you're actually feeling.

A daily mental-readiness check-in drives what the home screen shows next.

Logic, not just a log

Below a threshold, the reset card takes priority over everything else on the screen — Request Gear and Mindset Lab drop to secondary position. It's a small piece of logic, but it's the difference between an app that just records a number and one that actually responds to it.

Key flows

Mindset Lab, and gear without the awkward conversation.

Exercises named for the moment they're for; a structured request that replaces the DM-to-a-coach model.

Decisions

What the product should never require of someone.

The interface was the easy part. These calls came straight from the stigma problem.

What it had to solve forDesign response
Asking a coach in person for gear can feel like admitting you can't afford to play.→Request Gear is a form, not a conversation — anonymity on by default so a donor never sees a name.
A daily “how are you feeling” check-in only works if it changes something.→A low score reorders the home screen itself — the reset becomes the first thing you see.
Coordinators can't act on wellness data they have to go looking for.→Wellness alerts sit on the dashboard next to equipment requests — same priority, same glance.
Takeaways

Every screen has to earn its place twice.

Reflection

What I learned

Designing for two users on one platform means every screen has to earn its place twice — once for what a coordinator needs to act on, and once for what an athlete needs to feel safe enough to use at all.

Next steps

What's next

This is concept-stage: a design system and flows built solo in Figma, with no research behind it yet. Talking to real coordinators and youth athletes comes before any of this could ship.
